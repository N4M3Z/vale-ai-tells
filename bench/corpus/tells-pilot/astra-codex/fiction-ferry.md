Nora held the cake box level while the ferry rolled beneath her feet. The last crossing left at eleven forty, and the crew had already raised the ramp. Through the cabin windows, she saw a cleaner pushing water across the empty terminal floor.

The only dry bench outside stood beside the engine housing. A man sat at one end, his knees pressed against a canvas bag.

“Is that space free?”

He moved the bag between his boots.

“Unless the cake needs it.”

“The cake has enjoyed enough attention.”

She sat, keeping the box on her lap as they left shore.

For a while, they watched the city shrink. Orange lights stretched across the black water, then broke beneath the wake. Somewhere above them, a loose fitting tapped against a rail.

“Birthday?” the man asked.

“Retirement.”

“Congratulations.”

“That seems to be the agreed response.”

He glanced at her, then smiled when she smiled.

“Was it a good day?”

Nora considered the paper banner, the warm wine, the headmaster reading her name from a card.

“Parts of it.”

“What did you do?”

“School dinners. Thirty years.”

“That is a lot of dinners.”

“Yes,” she said. “It was.”

The engines took them east.

He introduced himself as Daniel. His bag contained work shoes, he explained, and a shirt that smelled of popcorn.

“The cinema by the station. I clean after the final screening.”

“I used to go there.”

“It still has the terrible carpet.”

“Red flowers?”

“Possibly flowers. Nobody can prove it.”

She laughed and felt the box shift against her.

He checked his phone. A photograph filled the screen: a baby asleep beneath a yellow blanket.

“Yours?”

“Eight weeks.”

“Are you getting any sleep?”

“Some. Usually when somebody needs me awake.”

He typed a reply, then returned the phone to his pocket.

Nora rested her palms on the cardboard lid. All evening, people asked about her plans.

“Travel,” they suggested. “A garden. You could finally learn Italian.”

She had agreed that these things sounded pleasant.

Beside her, Daniel studied the lights along the dark shore.

“What happens on Monday?” he asked.

“Nothing.”

“That sounds good.”

“I always caught the five fifteen. Before that, the four fifty, when they changed the timetable.”

He waited.

“I keep thinking somebody will telephone because they cannot find the big saucepan.”

“Will they?”

“No. I showed everyone where it was.”

She pressed a finger into the lid.

A gust lifted the edge of her coat. Daniel trapped it against the bench before it touched a puddle.

“Thank you.”

“Strong wind tonight.”

She opened the box. Pink icing covered the cake, and someone had piped THANK YOU NORA across it. The knife and two paper plates lay underneath a napkin.

“They bought it from the supermarket,” she said.

“Is that bad?”

“No. I like this one.”

She rarely bought anything that she could make at home.

“Would you like some?”

“I would.”

She cut through the lettering, separating her name from the thanks.

The ferry sounded its horn.

Daniel balanced his plate against the bag. He ate with his fingers, carefully catching crumbs in his other hand.

“My wife likes the icing best,” he said. “She leaves the sponge.”

“An inefficient arrangement.”

“I eat the sponge.”

“Then you chose well.”

He smiled with his mouth full.

Nora took a bite. The cake tasted of sugar and lemon flavouring. After an evening of handshakes, photographs, and speeches, hunger reached her.

“I ate nothing at the party,” she said.

“Too busy?”

“Cutting cake.”

Daniel looked at her plate.

“Take another piece.”

“I have barely started this one.”

“There is time.”

The ferry cleared the harbour wall. Waves slapped its side, and Nora braced her shoes against the deck. Daniel steadied the box between them.

For several minutes, neither spoke. She heard him lick icing from his thumb.

“Sometimes I stay here instead of going inside,” he said. “Even in winter.”

“To avoid the carpet?”

“To sit where nobody needs anything.”

She thought of the baby beneath its blanket.

“Just for the crossing,” he added.

“I understand,” she said.

He nodded. Beyond the railing, a channel marker flashed green. Nora watched it disappear behind them, then waited for the next one.

“Will you miss it?” Daniel asked.

“Some children said thank you every day. Others learned after a few years.”

“The children, then.”

“And the women I worked with. And knowing exactly how much rice to put in the pot.”

“How much?”

“Depends who is eating.”

He laughed.

She turned her plate towards the light. A crescent of icing remained. She scraped it with her finger.

“I will not miss the rice,” she decided. “But I might miss knowing.”

Daniel considered this.

“I check whether the baby is breathing about twenty times a night.”

“That part gets easier.”

“I hope so.”

The island appeared as a low bank of windows. Daniel brushed crumbs from his trousers and reached for his bag.

Nora cut another piece, larger than the first.

“For your wife.”

“She would like that.”

“Take the rest, if you want.”

She made the offer before she even thought about it.

Daniel glanced into the box.

“Do you want the rest?”

She looked at the uneven pink edge, the exposed layers of pale sponge.

“Yes,” she said. “Actually, I do.”

“Then I had better leave it.”

She wrapped his piece in the napkin. He held it flat against his palm.

They stood when the engine slowed. Along the quay, the night crew waited beside a stack of ropes.

“Enjoy your Monday, Nora.”

“I expect I shall be awake at four.”

“You can stay in bed.”

“I might.”

She knew she probably would not. There would be gulls arguing on the roof and milk in the fridge. She could make breakfast when she wanted it.

At the ramp, Daniel raised his parcel before turning towards the waiting buses.

Nora carried the box through the gate. The wind pressed at her back. She had half a cake left, enough for several days.
