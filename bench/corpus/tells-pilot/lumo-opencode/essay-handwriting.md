The envelopes arrive in bundles now, like something washed ashore. Bills, catalogs, a coupon for gutter cleaning. But sometimes, buried in the pile, there is one with handwriting on it, and my whole posture changes before I have even registered why. The pen pressed too hard in places. A crossed-out word. The stamp slightly crooked. Someone sat down for me.

That is the whole of it, probably, but it deserves a slower look.

A letter is slow in a way that almost nothing else in our lives is slow anymore. The writer cannot delete. If you change your mind mid-sentence, the evidence stays, a small strikethrough that says: I thought one thing, then I thought another, and I let you see both. Email flatters us into believing our first draft is our finished self. A handwritten letter admits the drafts were the self. My grandmother's letters from the 1970s are full of these seams. In the margins she wrote grocery lists. On the back of one she traced her hand, spread wide, so I could compare mine against hers at age eight. No formatting menu offers that.

There is also the matter of the hand itself. We have nearly lost the ability to recognize each other this way. For centuries, you could pick out a friend's handwriting across a crowded post office, the way you now pick out their walk or their laugh. My father writes in cramped capital letters, never cursive, because a teacher in 1962 told him his lowercase was disgraceful, and he decided, at nine, never to risk it again. I know this because he told me, but I also know it because every birthday card he has sent carries the scar of that classroom. Typography has no grudges. Handwriting is nothing but grudges, and pressure, and the particular fatigue of a wrist on a Tuesday.

Some people say they write letters because letters get kept. This is true in a way that is hard to overstate. My family has a shoebox containing a letter my great-grandfather sent from a lumber camp in 1911, four sentences long, informing his wife that the winter was " tolerable" and enclosing two dollars. Nobody in the family keeps texts. The medium itself makes the argument: this survived a century in a box under stairs, through floods and moves and indifference, because paper asks nothing. It needs no password, no operating system, no company that stays in business. You hold it up to a lamp and it works. A letter is the only message we send that does not depend on the sender still existing, or the infrastructure still running, or anyone remembering to pay for storage.

But I don't think permanence is really why the letter writers I know keep doing it. I think it is the obligation of the body. To write by hand is to spend something measurable: fifteen minutes, an ache in the knuckles, half a sheet of good paper you were saving. The recipient feels the spending. It is the difference between being handed a gift and being handed a receipt for a gift. When my friend Marcus was going through a divorce, he told me he received ninety texts and three letters, and that he reread the letters and never reread the texts. He could not explain it except to say the letters "took up room." They took up room in the envelope, on his nightstand, in his sense of what he was worth to people. Weight, literally.

There is a privacy to it that we have forgotten how to name. A letter passes from one hand to one hand. It is not scanned for keywords, not summarized by a machine, not surfaced at an anniversary by a service hoping to sell photo books. It is addressed, deliberately, to a single reader, and everyone else in the world is excluded by the physics of paper. In an era when nearly every act of communication is also an act of publication, performed on a stage owned by somebody else, the letter is one of the last rooms with a door. My mother wrote letters to her sister for fifty years, and when the sister died, the letters came back to her in a crate. She has not reread them. She says she does not need to; she knows what is in them; that is what writing them was for.

And there is the delay, which we treat as a defect and which is the point. Between the writing and the reading sits a stretch of days during which the writer's mood settles, the news ages, and the sentences do their slow work without the writer hovering. You cannot ask a letter "did you see this?" or watch the typing dots pulse. The letter goes out like a message in a bottle: thrown sincerely, aimed loosely, arriving whenever. Some of the best letters I have received answered questions I had stopped needing answered and raised ones I had not yet thought to ask. The slowness creates a strange intimacy across time. You are alone with someone who was recently alone, thinking of you.

None of this makes the handwritten letter superior, exactly. It is inefficient, bad for the wrists, environmentally expensive, and often illegible. My grandfather's last letters to me were shaky enough that I had to hold them at arm's length and guess. But those guesses are now among the things I own. I would not trade them for a clearer recording of his voice, though I have that too, on a phone I no longer own, in a file I cannot open.

So the people who still write by hand are not being nostalgic, or not only. They are insisting that some communications should cost the sender something visible, and should reach only one person, and should carry the marks of the hours they were written in: the smudge, the cramped capitals, the grocery list, the traced hand. A letter is proof that a body sat somewhere and thought of you, at length, without interruption, on paper that will still open a hundred years from now, in a lamp's plain light, no matter what becomes of us.
