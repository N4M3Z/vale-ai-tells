The last ferry of the night pulled away from the dock at 11:47, four minutes late, and the only two passengers in the forward cabin were a woman named Ruth Alvarez and a man who had not yet told anyone his name.

They did not sit together at first. Ruth took the bench nearest the window, the long one with the green vinyl cushions, because she liked to watch the harbor lights slide backward and dissolve. The man sat three rows behind her, near the door, with a duffel bag between his knees like a dog he was afraid would run off. The cabin hummed. Outside, the water was the color of an old photograph.

At the midpoint of the crossing, the heater under Ruth's bench began to knock, a rhythmic tap-tap-tap like someone trapped in the bulkhead, and she got up to move. That was how it happened. No mystery to it. The other long bench, the warm one, was the one the man was sitting on, and there was plenty of room, and this was a boat and not a city street, where the rules about proximity were stricter.

"Do you mind?" she asked, gesturing at the far end of the bench.

He looked up from his hands as if he'd forgotten other people existed. "No. Please. It's warmer down here anyway."

She settled in, took off her glasses, breathed on them, and put them back on. The lights of the far shore hung low over the water.

"Late one," he said. Not a question, exactly. An offering.

"I missed the ten o'clock," Ruth said. "I always miss the ten o'clock. My sister says I was born twenty minutes behind and never caught up."

He smiled at that, a small crooked smile. "I've never taken this ferry before. I didn't know there was a last one until the man at the ticket window laughed at me."

"That's Karl. He laughs at everyone. It's affectionate."

"You know the ticket man?"

"I've taken this boat four hundred times." She said it plainly, the way you'd say you had brown hair. "I work on the island and live in the city. Or I work in the city and visit the island. It depends on the week. I'm a bookkeeper for the boatyard, and they let me do it mostly from my kitchen table, but once a month I go over and make them face the numbers."

"They hide from their numbers?"

"They bury them," Ruth said. "I dig them up. It's archaeological work, accounting. You find out what a business actually wanted, versus what it said it wanted."

He laughed for real then, a short surprised sound. "I'm going to a funeral," he said, and then winced, as if the sentence had come out ahead of schedule. "Tomorrow. I mean today, technically. That's why I'm on the late boat."

"I'm sorry," Ruth said.

"Don't be. It was my father, and we hadn't spoken in eleven years, so it's a complicated kind of grief. Mostly I feel like I'm grieving the funeral I'm not entitled to." He rubbed his thumb along the strap of the duffel bag. "I keep telling people it's complicated and they keep saying they're sorry, and both things are true and neither helps."

Ruth was quiet for a moment. The ferry's engine dropped half a tone as it slowed for the channel, and the knocking from the abandoned heater three rows up seemed louder now, farther away.

"My brother died four years ago," she said. "We hadn't spoken in six. Not a fight. Just distance, the kind that accretes. One year becomes two becomes six, and then somebody calls you and says the word *aneurysm*, and you realize the distance doesn't close just because the person did." She looked out at the water. "So I understand complicated. Not your version of it. But the shape of it, maybe."

The man nodded slowly. "The shape of it," he repeated. "That's exactly right. Nobody teaches you that grief has a shape. You think it'll be one thing, a wave, and instead it's more like weather. It comes in fronts."

"Are you staying with family over there?"

"No. There's a motel. The Sea Lantern." He said it with the particular weariness of a man who has already checked in online. "I'll do the funeral, and then I suppose I'll drive to the house and decide what to do with forty years of somebody else's life in boxes."

"The house is yours now?"

"Half mine. My sister has the other half. We haven't spoken either, come to think of it." He laughed again, quieter. "It's not a twist, you know. Everybody thinks there'll be a twist. A secret. A letter. But mostly families just drift, and then someone dies, and you all stand around a casket doing arithmetic about whose fault it was."

"There's usually a letter," Ruth said. "It's just boring. Mine left a note about where the deeds to the storage units were kept. Sixteen storage units across three states, and not one sentence addressed to me. My sister got a paragraph about a wristwatch." She shrugged. "The deeds turned out to be worth more than the watch, so I've made my peace with being the practical one."

"Have you?"

"Mostly. It comes back at odd hours." She tapped the window glass, where her reflection had been floating over the dark harbor. "That's what this boat is for, honestly. You get on the last ferry with something on your mind, and by the far dock it's usually lighter. I don't know if it's the water or the routine of it. I've never figured out the mechanism. But it works often enough that I keep the schedule on my refrigerator."

The man considered this. "How long is the crossing?"

"Forty-one minutes. On clear nights you can see the lighthouse at Halfway Rock for about six of them."

They sat with that. The ferry moved through its small dark kingdom. Somewhere below, a crew member dropped something metal and swore, and the sound carried up through the deck plates like a memory of a factory.

"What was his name?" Ruth asked. "Your father."

"Walter." He said it carefully, testing it. "Walter Joseph Kessler. He was a machinist. He made parts for things you'd never notice unless they broke."

"A good epitaph."

"He'd have hated it. He hated being noticed. Speeches made him physically ill. At my grandfather's funeral he sat in the car." The man smiled at the vinyl seat in front of him. "You know, I drove eleven hours today, and this is the most I've talked to anybody in a month."

"You're doing fine," Ruth said. "Better than most. There was a man on this ferry in March who talked to me for half an hour about coolant. Just coolant. I knew his whole divorce by the end of it, filtered entirely through automotive fluids."

"I hope you get a raise," the man said. "You're very good at this."

"I'm a bookkeeper. People tell me things because I ask the second question. Everyone asks the first question. How are you, sorry for your loss, how was the drive. Nobody asks the second question."

"What's the second question?"

"It's different every time. Tonight's was about the name."

The far shore began to assemble itself out of the dark: first the ferry dock's green running lights, then the sodium glow of the parking lot, then the motel sign, which was not, tonight, lit in the shape of a lantern but flickering half-alive, the way such signs do. The Sea Lantern. The man watched it come toward them and exhaled the way a person does at the end of held breath they hadn't noticed holding.

The ferry's horn sounded once, fat and low, announcing them to nobody. Deckhands appeared at the bow lines. Ruth stood, gathered her coat.

"Walter Joseph Kessler," she said, as a formal sort of goodbye. "I'm glad I sat on your bench."

"It's not my bench," he said. "But thank you. Same to you." He hesitated. "I'm Sam, by the way. I realize I never said."

"Ruth."

They shook hands, once, like people concluding a meeting that mattered. Then the ramp came down, and the cold came up, and they walked off the boat into the ordinary dark of the far town, in slightly different directions, both of them a little lighter than forty-one minutes ago, though neither could have named the mechanism, and neither needed to.
