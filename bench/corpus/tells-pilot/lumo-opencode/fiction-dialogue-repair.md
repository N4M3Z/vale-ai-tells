"Something in the back," the customer said, leaning the bike against the counter.

"In the back wheel?"

"Maybe. Or near it. It started two weeks ago."

"Started how?"

"It makes a noise."

Dale rolled the bike the last foot onto the stand and spun the cranks. The wheel turned clean. "What kind of noise?"

"A bad one."

Dale waited.

"Like a clicking," the customer said. "Or a grind. Somewhere between those."

"When does it do it? Pedaling, coasting, braking?"

"Peddling? Sometimes. Mostly when I'm just riding along."

"That's pedaling and riding at the same time, so that doesn't narrow it much. Does it happen on hills?"

"No. Well, yes. Actually, now that you mention it, mostly on hills."

"Going up or coming down?"

"Up." The customer paused. "And also the other day, down. But that might have been the wind."

Dale lifted the rear wheel and spun it again. "Clicking when you climb, under load, that's usually the bottom bracket or the cassette. Could also be a pedal. Does it speed up when you pedal harder?"

"I honestly couldn't tell you. I'm usually busy being annoyed by it."

"That's fair." Dale grabbed a pedal and wiggled it. Tight. He checked the cranks side to side. No play. He pulled a rag and wiped the cassette down, looking for a bent tooth. Nothing obvious. "Has anything happened to the bike? A crash, a car rack, a pothole?"

"It fell over in the garage once. But that was months before the noise."

"Months before?"

"Well, weeks. Time moves weird."

Dale stood up. "Okay. I'm going to ride it around the block. You wait here."

"You're going to hear it just like that?"

"That's the plan."

He was gone four minutes. When he came back the customer was standing at the counter with his arms crossed.

"Well?"

"I heard nothing."

"Nothing? That's impossible. It does it constantly."

"It did nothing for me. Rode the hill on Elm, stood on the pedals, everything."

"Then you fixed it by touching it. It knows."

Dale smiled despite himself. "It happens. Machines perform. Tell me exactly what it sounds like. Click click click, or more of a creak?"

"A creak, actually, now that I hear you say it. Like a door opening. A haunted door."

"Every crank revolution, or twice per revolution?"

The customer looked at him like he'd asked him to describe the smell of Tuesday. "How would anyone know that?"

"Some people count."

"I'm not some people. Look, it's a creak, it's from the back area, and it's driving me insane. My wife says she can hear it from the porch."

"She can hear it from the porch?"

"Okay, she said she could hear me complaining about it from the porch. But she's tired of it too."

Dale rotated the rear wheel slowly, watching the rim pass the brake pads. He squeezed each spoke. Near the seatstays he stopped. One spoke gave a faint ting. He plucked its neighbors. One spoke had maybe half a turn of tension missing.

"Your rear wheel has a loose spoke. On climbs you flex the frame, the wheel rubs the brake pad just enough to creak and tick. Going fast downhill the wheel spins straighter, so it quits."

"That's it? A spoke?"

"That's likely it. Twenty minutes, no charge for the diagnosis, ten bucks to true the wheel."

The customer exhaled like a man released from prison. "I've been telling people at work my bike was dying. I was pricing new ones."

"Take the ten bucks and buy your wife flowers."

"The flowers will cost more than the repair."

"Cheaper than a new bike." Dale wheeled it to the truing stand. "Next time, film a video of the noise with your phone. I can diagnose half of them off a recording."

"You could have led with that," the customer said, sitting down heavily on the bench. "I've been describing sounds to you like a madman for fifteen minutes."

"You did fine. 'Haunted door' is going in my notebook."
