The public library is one of the few civic rooms left that does not ask you to buy something before you sit down. It is also, in too many cities, a room that locks its doors just as the people who need it most are getting off work. A branch that closes at five or six does not fail because librarians are lazy or because the books have lost their audience. It fails the schedule of ordinary life. If a city can keep one promise to people who work for a living, it should be this: the library stays open into the evening.

That is not a romantic claim about the smell of paper. It is a claim about time. The hours between nine and five are already spoken for. They belong to employers, to school, to the second job, to the bus that runs late, to the child who has to be collected. A parent who stocks shelves until seven cannot use a reading room that shut at six. A student who shares a two-bedroom apartment with three roommates does not get quiet until everyone else is asleep, and by then the only free internet left in the neighborhood is a closed building with the lights off. We have arranged the one public good designed for study, job applications, and unhurried reading so that it is available mainly to people whose days are already free.

Evening hours repair that without inventing a new institution. They take a building the city already owns, a staff it already employs, and a collection it already paid for, and they put those assets on the clock of the people who fund them. Anyone who has stood in a branch at 8:15 knows what later hours produce. The room fills with people finishing résumés, teenagers doing homework away from a loud house, new arrivals practicing English, and older patrons who would rather not walk home in the dark from a five o'clock closing. The demand was never absent. It was locked out.

The objection that deserves a straight answer is money. Later hours cost money. They mean more wages, more security, more heat and light, and, in buildings that are already tired, more wear. Library budgets have been cut, frozen, and "right-sized" for years, and directors are right to flinch at any proposal that sounds like a wish list. A serious case for evening hours has to survive that flinch.

It survives because the alternative is more expensive in a way budget hearings rarely count. A library open only in the daytime has already paid for the building, the collection, the catalog, and the broadband, and then refused the hours when working households can use them. That is not thrift. That is a stranded asset. The marginal cost of two or three more open hours is real, and a city should publish it in plain figures beside the cost of a new branch. If the figures are too high, the honest move is to choose, not to shrug and close at five. Close a little earlier one morning if the numbers demand it. Share evening duty across a system so that not every branch stays late every night, but every neighborhood has one that does. Staff the late shift as a normal schedule, not as leftover overtime piled on people who already worked a full day. Security is a design problem: sight lines, a single public entrance, a guard where the numbers say one is needed, and the same rules after dark that apply at noon. Evening access is part of the service, the way a late train is part of a transit system.

There is a second version of the money objection that is really an argument about fairness inside the building. Evening hours, critics say, will pull staff away from children's story time, reference desks, and the morning programs that retirees rely on. That worry is legitimate if a city simply stretches the same small crew across a longer day and calls it innovation. It is not legitimate as a reason to keep the doors shut. Story time at ten in the morning and a quiet room at eight at night are not rivals. They are the same mission meeting different lives. A branch that can do only one of them should say so, and should say which residents it has decided not to serve. Most branches, given a schedule written on purpose, can do both.

Evening hours will not save libraries from the internet or from e-books. People who want a PDF can get one at home. People who want a place that is warm, lawful, and free of a cover charge cannot. The terminal for a job application still matters when the home connection is a phone with a cracked screen and a data cap. The long table still matters when the apartment is a bedroom with a television in it. Print still matters to the patron who does not trust a screen with a form that decides the rent. Those needs do not expire at dusk. They get sharper, because dusk is when the rest of the day's rooms have already asked for a wallet.

A city that closes its libraries at the end of the business day has made a choice about who the public is. It has chosen the retired, the unemployed, the very young with a caregiver free to bring them, and anyone else who can slip away at two in the afternoon. The choice worth refusing is the one that stops there. Workers pay the levy too. Students in crowded housing pay it in rent and in time. Shift workers pay it on the weeks they are free only after supper. A library that cannot be bothered to be open when they are free has not made a neutral budget decision. It has said that their time is less civic than someone else's.

Keep the lights on until nine. Publish the cost. Staff the shift properly, and judge the result by who walks in after supper, not by who was already there at three in the afternoon. The books can wait on the shelf. The people cannot.
