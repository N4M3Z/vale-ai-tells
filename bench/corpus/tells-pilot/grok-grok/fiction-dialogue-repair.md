"It makes a noise," the customer said.

"What kind?"

"A wrong one."

Mara set the Allen key on the bench. The green hybrid hung in the stand, basket empty, bell cracked through the logo. "Front or rear?"

"Both. Or the middle. Is the middle a place a noise can be?"

"The frame is usually quiet. When do you hear it?"

"When I'm on it."

"Coasting, pedaling, or braking?"

"Those happen in a pile. I can't sort them and also stay on."

"Does it pull?"

"It thinks about left. Then it behaves."

"On the brakes, or all the time?"

"I only notice when I'm scared, and I'm scared on the brakes."

She turned the crank. The chain ticked once each time around. He lifted a finger.

"Not that. That one's old. That one came with the bike."

"All right. The old tick stays. What's the new one?"

"It arrived after the rain. Or after my sister. She borrowed it and laid it down. On the driveway."

"Which side?"

"The ground side."

Mara tried the rear skewer. The lever swung under two fingers. "This isn't shut."

"It looks shut."

"Shut is flat against the frame. You shouldn't flip it without meaning to."

"I was gentle. I didn't want to snap anything off."

"You won't snap it off by closing it. You can walk the wheel out of the frame by leaving it like this."

"So the wheel is the noise."

"The wheel might be the pull. I still don't know the noise. Tell me the hill."

He put his hands in his jacket pockets. "The hill by the school. I brake with the right hand. First it's a rub, small, like a card in the spokes. I checked. No card. Then it's louder. Then, once, it went quiet, and the lever came in close to the bar. I put a foot down at the bottom."

"Right hand is the rear brake."

"Then I have been doing it correctly since 2009. My sister will be sorry to hear that."

Mara pulled the rear lever. The pads chirped, dry and high.

"Family of that," he said. "Mine lives in a box. It only speaks when the street tilts."

"The pads are glazed. Shiny, hard. They squeal. After they heat they can fade, which means the stop gets weak. A lever that comes in too far is a stretched cable or a pad worn thin. I'll know when it's apart."

"Fade is a curtain word."

"Here it's a hill word. Your hill is long enough."

"I didn't say fade because if I pick the wrong word, you fix the wrong part and I pay for the sentence."

"You pay for parts and for time. The sentence is how I choose."

"Then I'll say it badly and you can sand it down."

"You already said it. Right hand, school hill, rub, then louder, then quiet, lever too close, foot down. That's the job."

"That was the last minute. The rest was me protecting the old tick."

"The old tick is protected."

She closed the skewer. The lever snapped to the frame.

"Oh," he said.

"That snap is shut."

"I have not been hearing that snap."

"You've been hearing a wheel free to shift, and a brake that argues, in the same week, because the bike was on its side and nobody set it back up."

"She said it was fine."

"Fine means she got it back to you."

He watched the lever, unhappy with how simple it looked. "When it's a toaster I can say flame. When it's a bike I only have a feeling, and a feeling sounds like I want you to repair my nerves."

"Say what changed, which hand, and whether it still stops. The nerves can stay in the jacket."

"If I describe it wrong at home?"

"Come back before the hill. Say: rear lever, louder, then quiet, too close to the bar. Don't wait to see if the quiet is bravery."

"The quiet was not bravery."

"I know."

He squeezed the lever once, the way a person tests a door that might be someone else's. "Rear brake. Hills. A rub that gets loud, then nothing, and the lever reaches. I can stop if I start early. I will not practice starting late."

"Good."

"I could have led with that."

"You led with a noise. We got here."

"What do I owe you?"

"Nothing on the skewer if you close it once more while I watch. The pads are the number on the board. Leave the school hill alone until they're on."

"My sister doesn't borrow it."

"Your sister doesn't borrow it."

"And the old tick, if it comes tomorrow?"

"The old tick can come tomorrow," Mara said. "We already know its name. Call me when something new won't say its own."
