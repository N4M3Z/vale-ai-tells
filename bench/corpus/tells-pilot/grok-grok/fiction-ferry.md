The last ferry left the city terminal at eleven forty. By the time it cleared the breakwater, the cabin lights had dimmed and the snack counter was shuttered. Passengers who remained sat inside near the engines, where the air smelled of diesel and wet wool. On the open deck, one bench faced the water.

Mara sat at the left end, coat buttoned to the throat, a canvas tote between her feet. She had meant to catch the seven o'clock. The visit ran long. Her mother showed her the new tiles, then the wedding photographs Mara had missed, then the drawer of good silver, as if silver could vanish between one Tuesday and the next. When Mara stood at last, the apartment windows were already dark.

She heard the footsteps before she saw the man. He wore a work jacket and stopped a short distance off, looking at the empty half of the bench and then at her.

"Mind if I sit?" he said.

"It's a public bench," Mara said. The words came out sharper than she liked, so she added, "Go ahead."

He sat and set a hard hat beside his boot, crown down, so it would not roll. Yellow paint flecked the brim. Sawdust and cold air came off his jacket.

They watched the wake. The city drew thin, a line of sodium lights. A gull paced the rail for a minute, then slanted away.

"Long day?" he said.

"Long visit. You?"

"Long job. We poured a slab on Mercer until the inspector signed the ticket. I missed the nine-thirty by four minutes." He held up the fingers, then let his hand fall, shy of the gesture. "I'm Cal."

"Mara."

"You from the island?"

"My mother is. I come when I can." She nudged the tote. "She sent soup. She thinks the city starves people on principle."

Cal gave a small real smile. "My landlady leaves bread on the stair if she hears me after ten. I tell her I've eaten. She leaves it anyway."

"Does it help?"

"It helps that she does it."

The horn blew once for a buoy neither of them could see. Spray ticked on the boards between their shoes. Cal slid the hat an inch farther from the wet.

"You close?" he asked. "With your mother."

Mara let the easy answer pass. "We do better in the kitchen than on the phone. She talks. I dry plates. If I start anything serious at the table, she remembers the oven. Tonight the tiles were an excuse. She wanted the daylight gone before I put my coat on."

Cal nodded as if the shape was familiar. "My father walks me to the truck and finds one more fault in the porch light. He was thirty years into that habit before he managed to say drive safe and stop."

"He said it?"

"Once. Last March. Then he went inside so I couldn't answer. I sat there like a fool." Cal thumbed a seam in his sleeve. "Bad knees now. He still gets on the ladder if I leave him unsupervised."

"You'll see him tomorrow?"

"If I call from this deck, he'll wait up. So I won't."

"I texted from the ramp," Mara said. "Told her I had a seat. She frets about the gap between the boat and the pier."

"That gap is a hand's width."

"Try telling her."

The engine dropped and settled. Inside, a child laughed once and was shushed. Cold came up through the bench. Neither of them moved to the cabin.

Mara opened the tote to occupy her hands and found the soup container, tape doubled, a note in block letters: heat low, stir once. A smaller box sat under it. She had not seen her mother add that one.

"Cake," she said.

"She would."

"I told her no sweets after eight."

"She filed that under suggestions."

Mara huffed a laugh. Two squares of spice cake, wax paper, corners crushed. She offered one across the bench.

"You should keep it."

"She packed two on purpose. Either I was not to finish both, or I was not to eat alone. She doesn't separate those ideas."

Cal took the square. The center was still warm. Clove, orange peel, more sugar than any adult needed. Mara had eaten this cake at twelve with harbor water drying on her cuffs.

"That's the real thing," Cal said. He folded the paper into a square small enough for a pocket. "You can tell her I said so. Or let her assume. She'll assume right."

"She will."

Ahead, the channel lamps lifted out of the dark in pairs. The ferry took its turn, and the bench shifted under them. For a second their shoulders nearly met. Neither apologized. There was nothing to apologize for. The boat had moved. They had stayed where they were.

"You think about moving back?" Cal asked.

"In January. Then I remember I need other people's noise. A radio through a wall. On the island, after nine, I can hear my own house too clearly."

"I gave the city a year," he said. "Came home when the ladder started. The truck was already aimed at the ferry. It was a Tuesday."

"Do you regret it?"

"Not most nights. The nights I do, I sit out here until my ears quit, and then I can drive."

A deckhand leaned out. "Ten minutes. Watch the ramp."

Cal lifted a hand. The door shut.

"You have a ride?" Mara asked.

"Truck's in the lot. You walking?"

"Eight blocks. The porch light will be on. I told her not to wait."

"Leaving a light is not the same as waiting," Cal said.

He stood, hard hat under his arm. "Thanks for the cake."

"Thanks for taking the other half of the bench."

"It was empty."

"Even so."

He nodded, the same small smile. "Get home all right, Mara."

"You too. If your father is still up, tell him the porch light can rest."

She watched the dock lights draw close.
