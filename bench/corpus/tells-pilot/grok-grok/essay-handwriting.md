Last Thursday a man in a canvas coat stood there longer than the act required. He had let the envelope go. He kept his hand on the slot, as if the door might swing back and give the thing up. It was cream paper. A corner had failed to drop, and on that corner the address showed through from the other side, each stroke driven hard enough to raise the fibers. When he walked off, the corner stayed out in the weather. I pushed it through. I read the name while I did it. A woman, a city two states east, no return address on the face.

I keep meeting that pause in people who could have sent the same words before a kettle boiled. A friend writes to her sister every Sunday from a rented room above a bakery. The paper was their mother's and is running out: pale gray sheets with a deckled edge, twenty years in a drawer, foxing along the corners where damp got in. A laptop sits on the table. She fills one page, sometimes two, and walks them to the post office before the counter closes at five. Her sister lives forty minutes away by train. They talk on the phone.

I asked her why she bothers. She looked at the fountain pen. "I like knowing where I was when I wrote it." The room, the hour, sugar scorching in the ovens under the floor. Once the ink is dry, the sentence has to stand. On the screen she revises until the feeling is gone and a clean report is left. On paper the first version goes into the envelope. A bad line gets one stroke through it, and the words stay readable underneath. Some Sundays the page carries three of those strokes and a blot where the nib rested. She mails it. Her sister once answered on a postcard of a municipal swimming pool, one line and no greeting: "I like the part you crossed out." They have never settled which part.

I kept the habit for a winter after another friend moved to a town with poor reception and a post office that sorted by hand in the afternoon. I bought a pad of thin white paper and a pen that cost more than the pad. The early letters were stiff. Weather, work, the price of eggs. That is what comes out when you are performing calm for a sheet of paper. By the fourth letter I was setting down sentences I would have erased on a screen. They stopped before any conclusion. A message wants to be finished before breakfast. A letter can leave the house unfinished. Whoever opens it is already in another day.

One of those letters said I had stood in the doorway of his old apartment after the movers left and could not remember which wall the bookshelf had used. It sounded, in the writing, like a mood. On the page it became a question about a room. He answered in pencil on the back of a grocery receipt. The shelf had been on the north wall, beside a crack he never repaired. He drew the crack, the outlet under it, and a small circle for the hook where his coat used to hang. I still have the sketch. The paper has gone soft along the fold. A gray mark sits in one corner, from a wet thumb. I was at the sink and forgot to lift my hand.

The waiting was the part I understood last. Three days in clear weather. Five when it rained. A week when the pass closed and the truck sat in a town I have never seen. During that time the letter changed its temperature. The night I mailed it, I wanted the envelope back. I walked past the box after supper. Pickup had been at four. I knew that, and I looked at the slot anyway. On the second day I hoped he would open it in a kinder hour than the hour that wrote it. His reply came on a Wednesday. I broke the flap at the kitchen counter and left ink on a white plate. The plate washed clean. The pages went into a drawer, beneath a screwdriver and the warranties I will never claim.

He reads each letter twice. Once in the post office lot, car door open, the engine still ticking as it cools. Once at his table, beside coffee he pours and then forgets. He answers the second reading. By then I have usually lost the exact shape of the sentence I was ashamed of. He has it in my handwriting, which slants down when I am tired and presses harder on any word I do not trust. He says he can see the tracing, the second pass darker than the first. When we type, the exchange ends before either of us stands up. There is nothing afterward to put in a drawer.

I passed the same blue box in the rain. I thought of the cream envelope, and of a woman tipping a page toward a lamp to see where the pen bore down. The man had waited to hear the door shut. Until that small sound, the writing was still his.
