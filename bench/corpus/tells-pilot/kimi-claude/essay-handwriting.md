The drawer sticks before it opens, and when it does, the smell comes up first: cedar liner, old paper, the faint mineral ghost of ink. Inside is a shoebox my grandmother covered in wallpaper sometime in the sixties, and inside the box are letters, bundled with a rubber band that snapped years ago and left two brittle commas of itself among the envelopes. I know these letters. I have read them in the way you read things you are not supposed to keep rereading, and still, when the drawer sticks and gives, I feel something in me adjust its posture.

Her hand was a slope to the right, the letters leaning like people walking into wind. She wrote on airmail paper so thin you could read the next sheet through it, and she never crossed anything out. That is the part I return to. Not the news in the letters, which was ordinary, the price of oranges, a neighbor's surgery, rain. What I return to is the absence of error, or rather the refusal to acknowledge it. Somewhere in the third bundle she writes my uncle's name with the wrong initial, a D where there should be an R, and she simply finishes the word and goes on. The mistake is still there, sixty years old, dry as a pressed flower. A text message would have eaten it. Here it sits, proof that a person was moving through time at the speed of her own hand and did not stop to fix herself.

I started writing letters again during a winter when the power kept going out. The house would go dark and the router's little constellation would die, and after the first hour of phone-battery rationing I would light a candle and find, with nothing left to refresh, that I had things to say to people. Real things, it turned out, the kind that had been dissolving into the general noise for years. I wrote to a friend in Lisbon who I had not spoken to properly in three years. I wrote to my aunt. I wrote to a man I owed an apology, and the apology took four drafts, and each draft existed, physically, and had to be dealt with, folded into the woodstove or kept, and the keeping or burning was itself part of the deciding. You cannot burn a draft of an email. There is nothing to burn, and so the deciding never quite happens, and the sending is too easy, and the apology arrives weighing nothing.

The pen matters more than I expected it to. Mine is a cheap fountain pen, the kind students use in Germany, and it scratches on the upstroke if I hold it wrong, which I do. The scratch is audible. Writing a letter, I can hear myself think, literally, a small rasping like a mouse in a wall. I have come to distrust silence in writing. The blinking cursor makes no sound and forgives everything, and I have noticed that what I type in silence comes out smooth and false, committee prose, while what I write with the scratch comes out crooked and mine. Other letter writers I have asked say versions of the same thing. A woman I know writes only with a pencil because, she says, she likes that it gets lighter in her hand as the letter goes on, the point wearing down, the words toward the end slightly fatter and darker. Her letters record their own fatigue. She can see where she tired.

Then there is the envelope, the stamp, the walk. Our mailbox is a quarter mile down the road and the walk back, empty-handed, is longer than the walk out. You have given something up. It will take four days to reach Lisbon, maybe six, and during those days the letter exists somewhere, in a sack, on a plane, in the hands of a Portuguese postal worker I will never meet, and the friend does not have it yet, and I cannot recall it, cannot add a follow-up, cannot soften it. The letter is gone in a way nothing digital is ever gone. I think people write letters by hand, partly, to recover that gone-ness, to say a thing and have it leave. We are drowning in retrievable words. Everything is saved and nothing is sent.

The reply, when it comes, arrives at the speed of weather. My friend in Lisbon writes on the backs of exhibition postcards, cramped, running out of room exactly where the interesting part starts, so that every letter ends in a parenthetical sprint. I hold the card he held. He pressed on this paper and the paper pressed back and it kept the shape of his pressure, the dents of his pen readable by fingertip in the right light. This is the thing people mean when they say letters are personal, though they usually say it vaguely, sentimentally. It is not vague at all. It is mechanical. Force, applied to a surface, at a distance of a thousand kilometers and six days, reaching my thumb. No screen does this. The screen shows me what he wrote; the card shows me that he was there.

I do not think the hand-written letter will survive as a common thing. It barely is one now. The shoebox in my drawer is an archive of a world that has already ended, and my own letters, stacked unsent or returned with wrong addresses, are a small eccentricity, like keeping chickens. But on the dark winter evenings when the candle is lit and the pen scratches and the mouse in the wall keeps time, I am not being eccentric. I am doing one of the oldest things a person can do: making a mark that cannot be unmade, and trusting the weather to carry it.
